The Political Economy of Imperial Accumulation

A collaborative investigation into how imperial accumulation is constituted, reproduced and transformed through economic and political relations.

How is imperial accumulation constituted and reproduced through interacting economic and political mechanisms, and under what conditions can those relations be transformed?

This initiative proposes a collaborative investigation into the historical processes of imperial accumulation, drawing on diverse and sometimes competing traditions of political-economic analysis.

It does not privilege unequal exchange, colonial drain, international value transfers or any other particular explanation as its theoretical starting point. Rather, it asks how the processes identified by different approaches may be historically related, where their explanations conflict, and what these disagreements reveal about imperial accumulation as a whole.

The programme is an open research proposition. Its questions, methods, priorities and forms of collaboration remain to be developed through scholarly discussion.

01 / The explanatory problem

Understanding imperial accumulation as a historical process.

Research on imperialism has developed important, sometimes competing explanations of unequal exchange, colonial drain, international value relations, monopoly, labour and resource appropriation, and uneven development.

These approaches do not necessarily investigate the same phenomena, operate at the same level of abstraction or share compatible theoretical assumptions. Their disagreements are substantive and cannot simply be resolved by placing different explanations alongside one another.

Yet the historical processes they seek to explain may be connected in ways that remain insufficiently understood when each is examined in isolation.

The initiative therefore asks how particular relations of production, exchange, appropriation and political authority constitute, condition and transform one another within the wider historical process of imperial accumulation.

The aim is not to reconcile competing theories in advance, but to investigate the relationships between their explanatory objects, including where existing theoretical formulations may require reconsideration.

02 / Lines of inquiry

Questions for collective investigation

Mechanisms and relations

How do different forms of appropriation, value transfer and economic dependence arise, interact and change historically? Where do explanatory frameworks converge, conflict or investigate distinct processes?

Conditions of reproduction

What political, institutional, productive and material capacities sustain relations of imperial accumulation? How are those capacities themselves produced, reproduced and reorganised?

Contestation and transformation

Under what historical conditions can established relations of appropriation and dependence be challenged, reorganised or displaced? How do alternative political and material capacities emerge and endure?

03 / Research approach

A shared problem, not a unified school.

The programme is organised around a common explanatory problem rather than agreement with any particular theory of imperialism. It welcomes substantive theoretical disagreement and competing interpretations of historical evidence.

Its central methodological proposition is that the relationships among different explanatory objects should be investigated rather than assumed. Historical connections do not, in themselves, establish the compatibility of theories used to explain them.

Research may combine theoretical investigation, historical inquiry, quantitative analysis, transparent model reconstruction, and the examination of political and institutional processes.

Particular attention will be given to distinguishing theoretical propositions, historical evidence and quantitative findings; documenting assumptions and limitations; and investigating the conditions under which different explanatory mechanisms operate.

The ambition is to develop a richer historical explanation of imperial accumulation through collective inquiry, rather than to establish agreement in advance.

04 / Research infrastructure and resources

Supporting open and collaborative research.

The initiative is at an early stage. Its research infrastructure and collaborative arrangements remain open for discussion.

One existing resource that could support this work is the Unequal Appropriation Toolkit, an independently developed, open research project documenting methods, datasets, assumptions and limitations in research on unequal exchange and economic dependence.

The Toolkit is not the theoretical foundation of this programme, nor does participation in the programme imply endorsement of unequal-exchange theory. It offers an existing technical and methodological resource that may be extended or adapted in response to collaborative research needs.

05 / Participation

An invitation to shape the research together.

This initiative invites discussion with researchers working on imperialism, political economy, historical development, international value relations, colonial appropriation, uneven development and political transformation.

Its purpose at this stage is to explore whether a shared programme of research could be developed around these questions, while preserving space for theoretical disagreement and different methodological approaches.

Possible forms of collaboration include theoretical discussions, historical case studies, working papers, datasets, reproducible calculations, methodological critiques and collaborative workshops.

No particular technical platform or programming expertise is required to participate in discussions about the proposed programme.

Researchers interested in contributing to the existing technical resources can also consult the Unequal Appropriation Toolkit contribution page .

The programme is currently a proposal, not an established consortium. References to existing research or resources do not imply the endorsement or participation of their authors.
